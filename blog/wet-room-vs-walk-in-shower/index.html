Wet Room vs Walk-In Shower: Which Is Right for You?

2026-03-05

Two good options for different rooms

Walk-in showers and wet rooms are often used interchangeably in conversation, but they're quite different in how they're built, what they cost, and how well they suit different homes and lifestyles.

This guide cuts through the confusion so you can make the right call before committing to either.

What's the Difference?

A walk-in shower has a defined shower area, typically a tray or tiled shower floor, with one or more glass screens or panels. Water is contained within the shower footprint. The rest of the bathroom floor stays dry.

A wet room has no tray and no defined enclosure. The entire floor is waterproofed and graded towards a drain. In theory, you can walk anywhere in the room, or shower anywhere, and water drains away. Often the whole room is fully tiled floor to ceiling.

Wet Room: The Pros

Completely open and accessible

For wheelchair users, people with mobility issues, or anyone who finds stepping over a tray difficult, a wet room makes the bathroom usable again. There's no lip, no threshold, no obstacle. This makes wet rooms particularly popular for accessibility retrofits and for homeowners thinking ahead to later life.

Sleek, high-end look

A well-built wet room with full-height tiles, a linear drain and a single glass screen looks expensive. It's one of the few bathroom upgrades that reliably adds to the perceived value of a property.

Space efficiency

Because there's no tray taking up a fixed footprint, a wet room works in almost any shape of room. L-shaped, awkward corners, unusually proportioned spaces, a wet room can be adapted.

Easy to clean

No tray edges to scrub. No silicone seal around a shower tray to regrout. The entire floor surface is one material, usually large-format porcelain, which wipes down easily.

Wet Room: The Cons

Higher installation cost

A wet room must be correctly tanked. Every surface that could come into contact with water needs a waterproofing membrane applied before tiling. The floor also needs to be screeded at a precise gradient towards the drain.

Done properly, this adds cost and time. Expect a wet room to cost 20 to 40% more than an equivalent walk-in shower installation.

The whole bathroom gets wet (obviously)

Some people love this. Others find it annoying, especially in a shared bathroom where someone wants to use the mirror immediately after showering. In a dedicated en suite or master bathroom, this matters less.

Requires precise installation

Wet rooms are very unforgiving of poor workmanship. An inadequate waterproofing job doesn't show up straight away. It typically takes 12 to 18 months for water ingress to become visible, by which point the damage to joists or walls can be substantial. Always use an experienced installer who can evidence their tanking method.

Walk-In Shower: The Pros

Lower cost

A good walk-in shower with tiled walls, a frameless glass panel and a decent tray costs much less than a full wet room. You're looking at £1,800 to £3,500 for the shower element of a bathroom refit, versus £3,500 to £6,000 for a comparable wet room.

The rest of the bathroom stays dry

This is a practical advantage in shared bathrooms. The vanity area, toilet and floor outside the shower stay usable while someone is showering.

Easier to retrofit

If your existing bathroom has a wooden suspended floor (common in Victorian and Edwardian properties), a wet room is significantly more complex to install correctly, screeding over wooden floors requires additional engineering. A walk-in shower with a standard tray avoids this issue.

Wide range of styles

From minimalist frameless panels to more decorative framed enclosures, the aesthetic options for walk-in showers are vast. A large walk-in shower with a ceiling-mounted rainfall head looks impressive without the complexity of a full wet room.

Walk-In Shower: The Cons

The tray requires maintenance

Silicone seals around shower trays eventually discolour and need refreshing. This is a minor maintenance job but one that wet rooms don't have.

Less accessible

Even a low-profile shower tray is a barrier for wheelchair users or people with significant mobility issues. If accessibility is a priority now or in the future, a wet room is the better long-term investment.

How to Choose

Go with a wet room if:

  • Accessibility is a priority (now or planning ahead)
  • You want the cleanest look, with no tray or step
  • It's a dedicated en suite or master bathroom
  • Your floor is concrete slab or ground floor

Go with a walk-in shower if:

  • You're working to a tighter budget
  • It's a shared family bathroom
  • Your property has wooden suspended floors
  • You want a faster installation

Floor Type Matters More Than People Realise

If your home has wooden suspended floors (as most pre-1970s UK properties do), fitting a wet room requires either:

  • A suitable tanking system designed for timber substrates, or
  • Building a platform or replacing the floor structure

This is entirely achievable but adds cost and complexity. A good bathroom fitter will assess your floor type before quoting. If they don't mention it, ask.
